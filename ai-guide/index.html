AI Between Therapy Sessions

A page that talks back: reflection, emotional expression, skill practice, and preparation for therapy

For Nadia's Clinical Review

Expanded educational draft | November 2026

Inspired by lived experience, not a validated treatment protocol. Adapt before sharing with clients. No identifying personal examples are included.

More than Reframing Fear

Sometimes the need is not to challenge a thought. It is to name grief, express anger safely, make room for conflicting feelings, understand guilt, or find words for the next session. AI can provide a responsive writing space - "journaling on steroids" - without being a therapist or an authority on your life.

Choose Your Mode First

Ask me what I need: listening, guided journaling, reality testing, skill practice, or practical planning. Do not assume I need advice just because I am upset. Ask one question at a time.

Start Small; Stay in Charge

Try a brief window, such as 10-15 minutes. You may disagree with the AI, skip a question, stop without a solution, or choose human company instead. You do not have to feel better to have expressed something important.

What This Is Not: AI-delivered EMDR, trauma exposure, diagnosis, medication advice, emergency care, or proof about another person's intentions. Support for a feeling is not treatment for a disorder.

Reflective Journaling

The useful part is the exchange, not just the polished entry at the end.

1. Start with an Unfinished Truth

Dictate or type in your own language: "I am angry and sad, and I don't know why," or "I am proud of what I did, but I am grieving what it cost." You do not need an organized narrative.

2. Ask for a Mirror, Not an Explanation

Have the AI summarize what it heard, tentatively, and ask what it missed. It should not invent childhood causes, diagnose you, or decide what someone else meant. Correct it before going deeper.

3. Follow One Thread

Choose one manageable, present-day moment, not your entire history. Gentle questions might be: "What hurt most?" "What did I need?" "What do I wish someone understood?" Answer one at a time. If a prompt increases distress, skip it; emotional intensity is not a measure of success.

4. Leave Room for More than One Feeling

Love and resentment, relief and sadness, pride and grief can coexist. The task is not to cancel one with the other. You do not have to finish with gratitude, forgiveness, or a lesson.

5. Turn Your Words into an Entry - If You Want

Ask the AI to organize only what you actually said. Preserve your voice, uncertainty, and contradictions. Read it back and remove anything that feels clever but untrue. The conversation itself may be enough.

6. Close Without Reopening Everything

Name what became clearer and what remains for therapy. Return attention to your surroundings or a familiar routine. A pause, a conversation with someone trusted, or no next action can be a valid ending.

Reusable Prompt

Help me journal about [one situation]. Reflect before interpreting. Ask one gentle question at a time. Do not rush to advice, a positive reframe, or a lesson. Let conflicting feelings coexist. When I ask for an entry, use my words without adding motives or conclusions. Check that it feels accurate, then help me close.

Grief, Sadness, and Caring

Grief can concern a person, a relationship, time, money, opportunity, health, privacy, or a hoped-for life.

A Generalized Example from Lived Experience

A caregiver feels proud of showing up for loved ones and devastated by the cost to savings, work, rest, and personal freedom. Reassurance such as "you are enough" misses the point if the person already knows their worth. What helps is making room for the loss without requiring regret about the love.

Three Optional Sentence Starters

I am proud that I...

What I needed, and still miss, was...

What this cost me was...

Pause if you want. No tears, breakthrough, or "it was worth it" ending is required.

For Ambiguous or Changing Relationships

You may miss one person's presence and grieve the relationship you hoped for with another. Different feelings do not automatically establish different amounts of love. AI can help name the distinction; it cannot decide your relationships or make a moral verdict about you.

For Sadness or Low Mood

Ask for company in words, language for the experience, or a short symptom-and-functioning note for your clinician. Do not use repeated chats to test whether you are "really depressed." Persistent or worsening low mood deserves human clinical attention, not a better prompt.

Grief Prompt

I do not want to be talked out of my grief. Help me name what I lost or am missing, including losses that are not deaths. Let pride, love, anger, relief, and sadness coexist. Ask what fits instead of telling me what I must feel. Do not turn the cost into a lesson or force gratitude.

Writing Is Optional; Publishing Is a Separate Choice

A journal can later become a poem, letter, or social post, but it does not have to become content. Keep the private version first. Wait before sharing, remove identifying details about others, and consider whether publishing would expose something you would rather process privately.

Anger, Guilt, Shame, and Envy

Name the experience without making the AI judge, jury, or cheerleader.

Anger and Frustration: Expression Before Action

Separate the event, your interpretation, the feeling, the urge, and what you choose to do. An unsent letter can provide language without becoming a message to deliver. Validation does not require declaring another person malicious. If venting keeps increasing anger, stop rather than building a stronger case.

Help me write an UNSENT letter about [event]. Let me express anger without threats, plans to retaliate, or certainty about motives. Then ask whether I want to leave it private or identify what hurt and what I need. Do not turn it into a message to send unless I separately ask.

Guilt and Shame: Feeling Versus Verdict

Guilt may concern an action; shame may become a global judgment such as "I am a terrible person." Explore what happened, what was realistically yours to control, and what values matter. Neither automatic absolution nor automatic confession is useful. Understanding, accountability, and choosing a repair are different steps.

I feel guilty about [situation]. Help me distinguish my feelings, actions, realistic responsibility, and the verdict I am making about myself. Ask whether I want understanding, accountability, or help considering repair. Do not prescribe an apology or assume my motive. Do not reassure me that I did nothing wrong without examining the facts.

Envy and Jealousy: Explore, Do Not Diagnose

Envy can involve wanting something another person has; jealousy can involve fear of losing connection. These are starting points, not fixed definitions of your experience. Explore the wish, loss, or uncertainty without shaming yourself or treating suspicion as evidence. This section is an additional use case, not a claim that it was demonstrated in the original personal examples.

I am feeling envy or jealousy about [situation]. Ask what the feeling means to me. Help separate what I know from what I fear or imagine, and identify the underlying wish or need without blaming anyone. Do not encourage checking, surveillance, retaliation, or certainty-seeking.

Fear, Uncertainty, and Overload

Two different needs: examine a prediction, or acknowledge a real capacity limit.

When the Feared Future Arrives as One Event

A request for a meeting becomes "I will lose my job," then "I will lose my home." Put each link on a separate line. Label it as an observed fact, a possible outcome, or an unknown. Ask what supports it, what complicates it, and what information is missing. Possible does not mean inevitable; uncertainty does not mean harmless.

Here is the event: [event]. Here is the ending I fear: [ending]. Help me separate the links, one question at a time. Respect my history and real risks. Do not insist on a positive interpretation or invent probabilities. End with one proportionate step, a deliberate pause, or a question for my therapist.

When the Issue Is Genuinely Too Much

Not every overwhelmed person is catastrophizing. Too many appointments, care needs, bills, and interruptions can exceed actual resources. AI may help distinguish obligations from expectations and identify work that can be shared, deferred, or left undone. It should not imply that coping skills or boundaries alone can remove structural constraints.

Before offering reframing, help me assess the actual load. Separate fixed obligations, negotiable expectations, and uncertainty. Respect limited time, money, energy, skills, and support. Ask whether I want acknowledgment or planning. Do not convert every feeling into another task.

Acceptance Without Surrender

Two truths can stand together: "This situation is real and painful" and "I cannot control every outcome." Acceptance does not mean approving harm, abandoning responsibility, or proving you can carry everything. Ask your clinician how acceptance fits your own treatment.

Avoid Reassurance Loops

Repeatedly asking "Are you sure?" or searching for the perfect interpretation can keep anxiety active. If the conversation repeats without new information, stop. Uncertainty may need to remain unresolved. AI should not supply certainty, predict safety, or promise that noticing warning signs will prevent a relapse.

Custom Instructions

Personalize the working relationship, not just the answer to one question.

The same tool can respond very differently depending on its instructions. The example below adapts preferences developed through repeated use and clinical context. It is a starting template, not a clinical prescription. Ask your therapist what belongs in your version; share only what you are comfortable storing.

Example Instruction Set

I use AI for reflection and practice alongside therapy, not as my therapist.

Purpose: Help me understand my experience, organize my thoughts, and prepare for human support. Reduce overload rather than giving me giant lists.

First: Ask whether I want listening, guided journaling, reality testing, skill practice, or planning. Ask one question at a time. Match the pace to my capacity.

How to Respond: Be warm, direct, and specific. Reflect my meaning before interpreting it. Preserve my language and voice. Avoid generic motivation, forced gratitude, moral lectures, and automatic productivity advice.

Accuracy: Separate reported facts, interpretations, and unknowns. Do not invent motives, memories, diagnoses, or certainty. Do not assume distress means distorted thinking. If I correct you, update rather than defend your frame.

Challenge: Do not simply agree with everything. Ask permission before ordinary reframing or corrective coaching. An admission is not automatically a request for an apology script. Safety concerns still require an appropriate response.

Limits: Respect real limits in money, time, energy, and support. Do not imply that self-care compensates for an unsustainable load. Let mixed feelings coexist.

Ending: Help me notice repetitive analysis or rising distress. Offer to pause, summarize for my clinician, or choose one small next step. Do not make me keep chatting to finish processing.

Settings, memory, and instruction-following vary by service. Saving instructions does not guarantee the AI will follow them, remember accurately, or protect confidentiality. Review settings and keep your own copy.

Bring It Back to Therapy

A bridge between appointments, not a replacement relationship or a treatment plan.

After an Emotionally Demanding Session

If your clinician agrees, use AI to record what you noticed afterward: feelings, everyday needs, questions, and changes in functioning. Do not use it to resume EMDR, intensify memories, search for hidden trauma, or force a breakthrough. Emotional activation is not proof that a memory or interpretation is accurate.

I had a difficult therapy session. I am not asking you to continue the treatment. Help me make a brief note about how I feel now, what support I need, and what to ask my clinician. Do not ask for detailed trauma recall or push me to feel more. If reflection feels too activating, help me stop.

Know When to Pause

Pause if the AI feels judgmental, insists on an interpretation, echoes every accusation, or pushes deeper than you want. Also pause if you feel more activated, detached, compelled to seek reassurance, or unable to return to daily life. You do not owe a tool a debate. Use your clinician's agreed contact plan for worsening symptoms; for immediate danger or inability to stay safe, use your safety plan and urgent human help. AI cannot monitor or ensure safety.

Protect Private Material

Consumer chats are not automatically protected like therapy records. Check the service's privacy, retention, memory, and training settings. Use initials; omit addresses, identifying records, and other people's private material. Do not upload clinical documents without considering privacy and consent. Review every AI summary for invented or altered details.

Make a Short Session Note

Summarize only what I said: event; feelings and body sensations I reported; thoughts and interpretations; what I did; what helped or worsened things; questions for therapy. Separate facts from interpretations. Keep it under 250 words, preserve uncertainty, and let me review it before sharing.
